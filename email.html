01 · Research question Rubric: research questions

Three questions, one experiment

A retailer emails customers to make money. Most analyses stop at "did the email work?". We go further: how it worked, and for whom. That tells a marketer whether to chase more buyers or bigger orders, and whom to send which email.

Q1

Does each email raise revenue per customer emailed?

Outcome: dollars spent in two weeks, averaged over everyone, buyers or not.

Q2

Is the lift from more people buying, or from buyers spending more?

Revenue per customer = (share who buy) × (average spend per buyer). We estimate each factor's contribution, with uncertainty.

Q3

Does it matter whether the email matches what the customer bought before?

Customers are grouped by their purchases in the past year (men's, women's, or both), recorded before the email was sent.

✓What these variables can establish

Causal effects of each email on visits, purchases and spend over two weeks: the email was assigned at random, so the groups differ only by chance and by the email.
Effects within groups defined by pre-email variables (past purchases), because grouping on something fixed before randomization keeps each comparison randomized.

✕What they cannot establish

Long-run effects: we only see two weeks, with no repeat purchases and no unsubscribes.
Why an email works (subject line? images? timing?): only the email type varies.
Effects of a visit on buying: visiting is itself caused by the email, so comparing visitors with non-visitors is not randomized.

03 · Concepts and approach Rubric: concepts and approach

Three course concepts, one per job

Lecture 8

Welch's t-test + Bonferroni

Answers
Q1: is the revenue difference real?
Why this method
It compares two means without assuming equal variances. The arms' spend variances differ.
Assumes
Independent customers; the CLT makes the mean difference ≈ normal. Checked in chapter 08.
Why the correction
We run 3 tests. Bonferroni keeps the chance of any false alarm ≤ 5%.
Lecture 11

Bayesian Beta–Binomial

Answers
Q1 and Q3: how likely is it that one email converts better than another?
Why this method
Buying is a yes/no outcome, a Bernoulli trial. The Beta prior is conjugate, so the posterior has a closed form, and it gives direct probability statements like P(θmen > θctrl).
Assumes
Customers in an arm share one buying probability θ; the prior is reasonable. Checked with four priors.
Lecture 9

Nonparametric bootstrap

Answers
Q2: how much of the lift is buyers vs basket, with a confidence interval?
Why this method
The buyer and basket ratios are ratios of ratios. No simple formula gives their standard error; resampling does.
Assumes
The sample represents the population; customers are independent; each arm is resampled separately, as randomized.

Notation (as in the lecture notes)

nT, nCcustomers in the email (treatment) arm and the control arm
X̄T, ST2sample mean and unbiased sample variance of spend in arm T
μT, μCtrue mean revenue per customer; H0: μT = μC vs H1: μT ≠ μC
θ, h, nbuying probability; number of buyers h among n customers

p, mshare who buy; mean spend per buyer (so μ = p·m)

04 · Concept 1 · Lecture 8 · Welch's t-test and p-hacking

Both emails raise revenue

What we do

Compare mean spend per customer between two arms, and ask whether the gap is bigger than chance would produce.

In symbols

TW = (X̄T − X̄C) / √(ST²/nT + SC²/nC). Under H0, TW ≈ t with Welch–Satterthwaite df.

How to read the chart

The black curve is what TW looks like if the email does nothing. The coloured line is what we observed. Far into the shaded tails means very unlikely under H0.

Watch out

Every extra test is another chance of a false alarm. Press "Run 20 fake tests" below to see it happen.

Three tests, one correction

Bonferroni: reject H0 when p ≤ α/m, with m = 3 tests (equivalently, multiply p by 3).

Splits the no-email group at random into two halves, 20 times. Nobody got an email, so any "significant" result is a false positive.

Why we correct

Fake tests run so far0
Batches with ≥1 false positive at α = 0.05–
…with Bonferroni α/20–

Theory: with 20 tests at α = 0.05, 1 − 0.9520 ≈ 64% of batches contain a false discovery. Bonferroni brings that back to about 5%.

TakeawayBoth emails raise revenue per customer, and that survives the correction. "Men's beats women's" on revenue does not: p = 0.03 alone, 0.09 after correcting for 3 tests.

05 · Concept 2 · Lecture 11 · Beta–Binomial updating

Feed the posterior, watch it learn

What we do

Treat each arm's buying probability θ as unknown, start from a prior belief, and update it with every customer we see.

In symbols

θ ~ Beta(α0, β0); after h buyers among n: θ | x ~ Beta(α0 + h, β0 + n − h). Add buyers to α, non-buyers to β.

How to read the chart

Each curve shows the plausible values of θ for one arm. Narrow means certain; curves that do not overlap mean a clear difference.

Try this

Press ▶ Play: the curves start flat and sharpen as customers arrive. Switch to "Sceptical": the answer hardly moves, so the data, not the prior, drive it.

Monte Carlo from the posteriors

TakeawayOn conversion, the men's email beats both the control and the women's email with posterior probability above 0.999, under every prior we tried. Buying is a 0/1 outcome, far less noisy than dollars, which is why it separates the emails when revenue could not.

06 · Concept 3 · Lecture 9 · Nonparametric bootstrap

Split the lift: buyers × basket

What we do

Split the revenue ratio into a buyer effect and a basket effect: μT/μC = (pT/pC) · (mT/mC).

The algorithm

For b = 1…B: resample each arm's customers with replacement, recompute all three ratios → θ̂*(b). The 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles give a 95% CI.

How to read the chart

Each histogram is the spread of one ratio across resamples; the shaded band is its 95% CI. The dashed line at 1× means "no effect".

Why not a formula?

A ratio of ratios has no simple standard error. The bootstrap needs only the data and a computer.

TakeawayRevenue roughly doubles, and essentially all of it comes from more buyers. The basket ratio's interval includes 1: buyers spend the same with or without the email.

10 · Interpretation and limitations Rubric: interpretation and limitations

What the data can and cannot establish

What we conclude, and why it is credible

Causal, not just correlation. The email was randomized (balance check passed), so differences between arms are effects of the email.
Robust across methods. Welch (frequentist), Beta–Binomial (Bayesian) and the bootstrap agree on direction and size.
Uncertainty is reported. Every effect has a CI or credible interval, and multiple testing is corrected.
Honest inconclusive results. Men's vs women's on revenue, and the basket effect, are not distinguishable from zero.

Limits

Two-week window: no long-run, repeat-purchase or unsubscribe effects.
Truncated spend: floored at $29.99 and capped at $499, so the basket estimates are slightly compressed.
Few buyers (578): "no basket effect" means "no large one". The CI allows about ±20%.
Exploratory subgroups, and one retailer in 2008: results may not generalize to other firms or years.
Constant θ per arm is a simplification: buying probability surely varies by customer.
