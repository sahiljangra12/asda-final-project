Mission 01 · A real experiment on 64,000 shoppers

Do marketing emails
make money? How?

An online store emailed its past customers. Every dot in the picture is one customer; the coloured ones bought something in the next two weeks. We use this experiment to answer three questions.

1Does an email raise sales?
2How? By getting more people to buy, or by making buyers spend more?
3For whom? Does the email have to match what the customer usually buys?

Why we can trust the answer

A coin flip decided who got which email

The store split 64,000 past customers into three groups completely at random. So the groups were the same kind of people before the email, and any difference afterwards was caused by the email, not by who was in the group.

Show the statistics

Method: Welch's t-test (Lecture 8). Compares two group averages without assuming equal spread. H0: μemail = μcontrol.

TW = (X̄T − X̄C) / √(ST²/nT + SC²/nC)

Bonferroni correction for 3 tests: each p-value is multiplied by 3 before comparing with 0.05.

Bars: average dollars per customer, buyers and non-buyers together. Black lines: 95% confidence intervals, the range the true value very likely lies in.

Not fooling ourselves

Test enough things and something always looks "significant"

Try it: the button runs 20 fake experiments in which nobody gets an email. We just split the no-email group in two at random. Any "significant" result is a false alarm.

!Press the button. On average, about 1 in 20 fake tests passes the usual 5% bar just by luck.
✓So we raise the bar when running several tests (Bonferroni). Both emails still pass. "Men's email beats women's email on revenue" does not: p = 0.03 alone, 0.09 after correction. We do not claim it.

Each dot is one fake experiment's p-value. Orange dots fall below 0.05: a false alarm at the usual bar. The black dashed line is the stricter Bonferroni bar.

Finding 2 · Question 2 · the headline

More buyers, not bigger baskets

Out of every 1,000 customers, about – bought without an email and – bought after the men's email. Each buyer spent about the same, –, either way.

✓Checked 10,000 times. We rebuilt the experiment from the data 10,000 times by resampling customers. Buyers always rose; the basket stayed near 1× (no change).
Show the statistics

Method: nonparametric bootstrap (Lecture 9). Revenue per customer μ = p · m (share who buy × mean spend per buyer), so μT/μC = (pT/pC)(mT/mC).

For b = 1…B: draw nT customers with replacement from the email group and nC from control, then recompute all three ratios. The 95% CI is the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of the B replicates. There is no simple formula for the SE of a ratio of ratios; the bootstrap needs none.

Finding 3 · Which email is stronger?

The men's email wins more buyers

Press Play. We start knowing nothing about each group's buying rate (flat curves) and learn as customers arrive one by one. The curves narrow and pull apart. The men's email ends up clearly ahead.

✓– probability that the men's email turns more customers into buyers than the women's email. This holds even if we start out sceptical, assuming emails do nothing.
Show the statistics

Method: Bayesian Beta–Binomial updating (Lecture 11). Each customer buys (1) or not (0) with probability θ.

Prior θ ~ Beta(α₀, β₀) ⇒ after h buyers of n: θ | x ~ Beta(α₀ + h, β₀ + n − h)

P(θmen > θwomen | data) is estimated by drawing from both posteriors (Monte Carlo) and counting how often one exceeds the other. On revenue the emails could not be separated, but buying is a yes/no outcome, much less noisy than dollars, so it separates them.

– · Curve height = how plausible each buying rate is, given the customers seen so far.

Finding 4 · Question 3 · for whom?

Men's email works on everyone. Women's email doesn't.

We split customers by what they had bought in the year before the email, then asked within each group: did the email raise the share who bought?

Show the statistics

Same Beta–Binomial comparison as Finding 3, run inside each past-purchase group. Numbers are the extra buyers per 100 customers (posterior mean), with the probability that the email helped at all.

These six comparisons were not planned in advance, so we treat them as exploratory. A Welch test on revenue with Bonferroni correction (6 tests) agrees: the women's email shows no effect on past men's buyers.

Why you can believe this

Four reasons to trust the result

✓

It was randomized

A coin flip chose the email, so the groups differ only by the email. That makes these causal effects, not just correlations.

✓

Three methods agree

A classical test (Welch), a Bayesian model and the bootstrap use different maths and reach the same conclusion.

✓

Our shortcut holds

Welch's test assumes averages behave normally, even though 99% of customers spent $0. The bootstrap error, –, matches the formula, –.

✓

Beliefs don't matter

Four different starting beliefs, including a sceptical one, give the same answer: P > 0.999.

So what should the store do?

Send the men's email widely: it brings in new buyers in every customer group. Do not expect emails to make people spend more per order. They change whether people buy, not how much.

What this cannot tell us

Only two weeks: we do not see repeat purchases or unsubscribes.
Spend is capped at $499 and starts at $29.99, so basket sizes are slightly squeezed.
Only 578 buyers: "no basket effect" means no large one (±20%).
One store in 2008; other stores and years may differ.
