Mission 02 · Written-report dataset · UCI Concrete Compressive Strength · 1,030 laboratory tests

Abrams' law,
107 years later

In 1918 Duff Abrams said one number sets the strength of concrete: water divided by cement. The curves above are that law, fitted to our data, at five curing ages. We test how far it goes, and what the recipe adds for mixtures the model has never seen.

01 · Research question Rubric: research questions

Is a 1918 law still enough?

Engineers design concrete with a rule of thumb: less water per kilogram of binder means stronger concrete. Modern mixes also replace some cement with industrial by-products (slag, fly ash) to cut cost and CO₂. Instead of just "predicting strength", we test a physical law and measure what it misses.

Q1

How much of the variation in strength does the law explain, and how precise is its slope?

Abrams: f = A / Bw/c, so log f is linear in the ratio. We use the water-to-binder ratio w/b plus curing age.

Q2

Do the individual ingredients add predictive information beyond the law, for mixtures never seen in fitting?

Judged on held-out mixtures, not on the data used to fit.

Q3

Left to choose freely, does a lasso pick the water–binder ratio?

A data-driven check of the physics: the lasso sees both ratios and all seven raw amounts.

✓What these variables can establish

Associations between recipe, age and strength across 427 lab recipes, and how well they predict new recipes.
Whether a simple physical law captures most of that association.

✕What they cannot establish

Causal effects of changing one ingredient: recipes were designed, not randomized, and ingredients move together.
Lab-to-lab differences: the data pool several sources, and curing conditions are not recorded.

03 · Concepts and approach Rubric: concepts and approach

Fit, check, choose, shrink

Lectures 12–13

Least squares + diagnostics

Answers
Q1: the law's slope, its SE and R².
Why this method
On the log scale the law is linear, so OLS estimates Abrams' constant directly: B = e−β₁.
Assumes
Linear mean, constant variance, independent errors. Checked with residual plots, leverage, Cook's distance and κ(XᵀX).
Lectures 14–15

AIC, BIC, grouped K-fold CV

Answers
Q1–Q2: how curved the age effect is, and which model predicts best.
Why this method
R² always rises with more terms. AIC and BIC penalize complexity, and CV measures error on data not used to fit.
Our twist
Folds are whole recipes, so a recipe never appears in both training and validation.
Lectures 14, 16

Ridge and lasso

Answers
Q2–Q3: does shrinkage help a 44-term model, and which variables does the lasso keep?
Why this method
Raw ingredients are nearly collinear. Penalties stabilize the coefficients, and the lasso sets some exactly to 0.
Bayesian view
Ridge = MAP with N(0, τ²) priors (λ = σ²/τ²); lasso = MAP with Laplace(0, b) priors (λ = 2σ²/b).

Notation (as in the lecture notes)

Yi = log firesponse: log strength of test i, i = 1, …, n
ri, aiwater/binder ratio; ai = log(agei/28)
y = Xβ + εmodel in matrix form; X is n × (p+1) with a column of 1s
β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀyleast squares; Var(β̂ | X) = σ²(XᵀX)⁻¹, σ̂² = RSS/(n−p−1)
hii, Dileverage (diagonal of H = X(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ); Cook's distance
AIC, BIC−2 log L̂ + 2k and −2 log L̂ + k log n
λpenalty weight for ridge (‖β‖₂²) and lasso (‖β‖₁)

Hypothesis we test

H0: β1 = 0 (water/binder has no association with log strength) vs H1: β1 ≠ 0, using t = β̂1 / SE(β̂1).

Different from Mission 01

Mission 01 is inference on a randomized experiment: tests, a Bayesian posterior, the bootstrap. Here the methods are regression, model selection and regularization. Only basic summaries and plots are shared.

06 · Concept 2 · Lectures 14–15 · AIC, BIC, grouped K-fold CV

How curved is the age effect?

The question

Which degree d of the log-age polynomial should the law use?

Three referees

AIC = −2 log L̂ + 2k; BIC = −2 log L̂ + k log n (a harsher penalty); 10-fold CV = average squared error on held-out folds.

How to read the charts

Lower is better. For CV, the bars are ± 1 SE: models within one SE of the best are statistically tied.

Why grouped folds

One recipe tested at 3, 28 and 90 days must not sit in both training and validation, or the error looks falsely small.

Folds are whole recipes. Training recipes only.

AIC and BIC − minimum

CV mean squared error ± 1 SE

Three criteria, three answers

TakeawayThe criteria disagree (AIC: 6, BIC: 2, CV one-SE rule: 1) because the differences are tiny. We use BIC's d = 2: extra age terms do not improve prediction.

07 · Concept 3 · Lectures 14, 16 · Ridge and lasso

Turn the penalty dial

What we do

Minimize ‖y − β01 − Xβ‖² + λ·penalty. Ridge uses ‖β‖₂² (shrinks everything); lasso uses ‖β‖₁ (sets some coefficients to exactly 0).

How to read the bars

Standardized coefficients at the current λ. Grey bars are raw ingredients; coloured bars are w/b, w/c and age.

How to read the path

Each line follows one coefficient as λ changes; the black marker is your λ. The bottom chart is grouped CV error: λ is chosen where it is lowest.

Try this

Lasso, drag λ to the right: variables drop out until w/b and age are almost all that is left.

Coefficient path · marker = current λ

Grouped CV error vs λ

TakeawayThe lasso picks w/b first (with age), ahead of Abrams' w/c and every raw ingredient. For prediction, CV prefers λ ≈ 0: with 800+ tests and 44 terms there is little variance to trade for bias. That is an honest null result.

Swap cement, wait, compare

What it shows

Predicted strength over a year for an all-cement binder (black) and for one where part of the cement is slag or fly ash (colour), at the same w/b.

The physics

Slag and fly ash react slowly (pozzolanic reaction). Positive "share × log-age" coefficients mean they catch up later.

11 · Interpretation and limitations Rubric: interpretation and limitations

A 1918 law still does most of the work

Why the results are credible

Validated out of sample on whole recipes never used in fitting, with CV and test errors agreeing.
Assumptions checked: the log scale removes curvature and negative predictions; no single test dominates (Cook's D).
Honest SEs: the 28-day subset (one test per recipe, so independent) gives the same slope, −2.68 (SE 0.13).
Three methods agree: OLS, information criteria and the lasso all point to w/b + age as the core.

Limits

Pooled labs: curing and testing conditions are unrecorded; some of the error may be lab differences.
Correlated tests: repeated ages of one recipe make full-data SEs optimistic.
Not causal: slag and fly-ash effects are associations conditional on w/b and age.
Variance not constant: young concrete is relatively more variable, so we rely on out-of-sample error.
Back-transform: exp(predicted log f) estimates the median strength, not the mean.

At the board · Normal equations

RSS(β) = (y − Xβ)ᵀ(y − Xβ)
∂RSS/∂β = −2Xᵀ(y − Xβ) = 0
⇒ XᵀXβ̂ = Xᵀy ⇒ β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy

Abrams: log f = log A − (w/c) log B, so B̂ = e−β̂₁.

At the board · AIC for regression

σ̂²MLE = RSS/n
−2 log L̂ = n log(RSS/n) + n log 2π + n
AIC = −2 log L̂ + 2k, BIC: k log n

k counts every coefficient plus σ².

At the board · Lasso update

ρj = zjᵀ(y − Σk≠j zkβk)
βj ← S(ρj, λ/2) / zjᵀzj
S(ρ, t) = sign(ρ) · max(|ρ| − t, 0)

Ridge: β̂ = (ZᵀZ + λI)⁻¹Zᵀy.
